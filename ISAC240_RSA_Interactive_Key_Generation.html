Important: n and z are calculated results—Bob cannot choose them independently after selecting p and q. In this lesson, z = φ(n) because p and q are distinct primes.

2 · Bob selects the public exponent e

We use the standard teaching rule 1 < e < z, and gcd(e, z) = 1. That means e and z share no positive factor except 1.

All valid values of e for the selected p and q:
Why this choice works

When z = 24, the standard values are 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23. Values such as 29 and 31 are also mathematically usable if a wider bound is allowed, but this interactive page follows the usual 1 < e < z convention.

3 · Bob calculates the private exponent d

Find a number d satisfying e × d mod z = 1. The smallest positive solution is the modular multiplicative inverse of e modulo z. Adding z gives other equivalent positive exponents.

What about e = 29 and e = 31?

They are coprime to 24 and can mathematically be used as exponents with suitable matching d values. However, they are not in the conventional 1 < e < φ(n) list because 29 and 31 exceed 24. The dropdown uses the conventional range so students can clearly enumerate all the valid options under that rule.

e × d mod z = 1.

05
Alice encrypts

c = me mod n using Bob's public key.

06
Bob decrypts

m = cd mod n using his private key.

Teaching safety note: These small numbers illustrate textbook RSA only and are completely insecure. Real-world RSA uses suitably large keys and secure padding such as OAEP. Also, not every variable has a finite independent list of choices: primes are unbounded, n and z are derived, and equivalent private exponents can differ by multiples of z.
